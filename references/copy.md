# Build the message from facts

Read [research](research.md), [the offer brief](offer-brief.md), and [plain writing](writing.md) for a full copy job. Reuse filled fields for a small edit.

## Decide what this piece must do

Name the buyer, where they came from, what they already know, the one choice they face, and what happens next. A page that sells a call needs to explain the call. A checkout needs clear terms. A product screen needs to help with the task at hand.

List the reader's questions in likely order. Back that order with customer evidence or label it as a proposal. For each question, choose the smallest useful answer and the proof it needs.

## Write the argument

Explain the problem in the reader's words. Show what the product does and why that may help. Link features to useful outcomes with a reason. Say what work the buyer still needs to do. Address the doubts that the research actually found.

Do not invent why earlier attempts failed, a secret mechanism, or hidden fears. If those points matter and the sources do not answer them, ask. For a claim with high stakes, use the limits check and the project's claim rules.

## Choose the opening

Use the opening that fits the reader's starting point:

| Reader's starting point | Useful opening | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Does not know the product | State the job or problem clearly | Avoid jargon and unsupported promises |
| Comparing ways to solve it | Explain how this option works and where it fits | Use a fair comparison |
| Knows the offer | Answer the remaining doubt or show the next step | Do not repeat a long introduction |
| Already bought | Confirm what they have and help them use it | Do not imply they must buy again to get value |

Write a few options when the message is unsettled. Name the question each answers. Do not produce dozens of near-copies as a substitute for research.

## Make the page carry the argument

Use the needed parts, in the order the buyer needs them: clear promise, view of the product or process, benefits, proof, terms, limits, questions, and action. Page length follows the decision. Do not force every page into one formula.

A headline must stay true when read alone. Supporting text should add information. A button should name the next action or result without hiding a charge or commitment. Answer curiosity you create; do not hide essential terms behind a video or extra click.

Use testimonials only when real, allowed, and relevant to the claim. Explain the group and limits behind a result. A best-case story does not prove a typical result. Do not make up a guarantee, review count, client logo, or countdown.

## Write exact text for every state

Include headings, body, labels, help, buttons, loading, empty, error, success, and plan branches. An error should say what went wrong and what to do next without blaming the user. Keep labels stable across the flow.

| Change / location / state | Exact text | Source or proposal | Claim support | Token source, format, and fallback |
|---|---|---|---|---|

List every token. For example, `Hi {first_name}` can become `Hi there` when the name is missing. Check long names, no name, no amount, missing date, and late data. Never print raw token syntax in the finished screen.

## Check the copy

Check facts and permission first. Then check clear meaning, proof, message match, action, and voice. If the words are clear but the offer is not known, stop the affected copy and ask. A writing pass cannot settle a price, approve a claim, or prove a strategy.
